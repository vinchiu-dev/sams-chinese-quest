Why Does the Moon Change Shape?

The Moon is always the same round ball. Here's the sunny secret behind crescents, half moons and full moons.

Look up tonight. Maybe the Moon is a big round ball. Maybe it's a skinny smile. Next week it looks different again. Is something taking bites out of it?

Here's a surprise: the Moon is the same round ball every single night. What changes is how much of its sunny side we can see. Let's find out how.

1The Moon makes no light

The Sun is a giant ball of hot, glowing gas. It makes its own light. The Moon is different. It's a cold ball of rock and dust that makes no light at all.

Moonlight is sunlight that bounced off the Moon.

So why does the Moon shine? Sunlight hits the Moon's surface and bounces off. When light bounces off something, we say it reflects. Your bike reflector does that with car headlights. 🔎 See a photo of the full Moon

2Half is always lit

Hold a ball near a lamp in a dark room. The side facing the lamp is bright. The side facing away is dark. It's always half and half.

The Moon works the same way. The Sun always lights the half of the Moon that faces it. The other half is dark. Earth is like that too. Right now, half of Earth is having daytime and the other half is having night.

Half of the Moon is always lit. Always.

3A month-long trip around Earth

The Moon doesn't sit still. It travels in a big circle around Earth. That path is its orbit. One lap takes about 29 and a half days, about 4 weeks. The word “month” even comes from the word “moon.”

As the Moon goes around us, we see it from a different side each night. Sometimes we see the whole sunny half. Sometimes just a sliver. Sometimes only the dark half, and the Moon seems to vanish. ▶ Watch it: the Moon's trip and its phases (SciShow Kids, 3:48)

The Moon's shape never changes. How much of its sunny half we can see does.

Because the Moon keeps moving, it rises about 50 minutes later each day. On lots of days it's up while the Sun is still up. Look for it some afternoon!

4A name for every shape

Each look is called a phase. The phases come in the same order again and again. Something that repeats like that is a cycle, and its order is a pattern you can predict.

It starts with the new moon, when we can't see it. Then comes a thin crescent, curved like a banana. Next is the first quarter, which looks like half a circle. It's called a quarter because the Moon is a quarter of the way around. Then comes gibbous (say GIB-us), more than half. Then the full moon! After that, it all runs backward. 🔎 See a photo of a crescent Moon

Waxing means growing. Waning means shrinking.

Think in fractions. One lap is about 4 weeks, so each quarter of the trip takes about 1 week. New moon to full moon is half the trip, about 2 weeks.

5It's not Earth's shadow

Lots of people think the dark part of the Moon is Earth's shadow. It isn't! During the phases, Earth's shadow misses the Moon. The dark part is just the Moon's own night side. ▶ Watch it: why Earth's shadow usually misses the Moon (NASA, 1:58)

But Earth's shadow does land on the Moon sometimes. That's called a lunar eclipse. It can only happen at full moon, when the Sun, Earth and Moon line up in a straight row. It happens only a few times a year, and the Moon can turn a coppery red. 🔎 See a photo of a lunar eclipse

Phases: we see part of the sunny side. Eclipse: Earth's shadow covers the Moon.

One More Thing

Some dung beetles use the Moon to steer. At night they roll their balls of dung (that's their food!) in a straight line by watching the moonlight in the sky. On nights with no Moon, scientists found some can even steer by the Milky Way. 🔎 See a photo of a dung beetle

Stay Safe

Looking at the Moon is safe, even with binoculars. Looking at the Sun is not. Never look straight at the Sun, not even for a second, and never through binoculars or a telescope. It can hurt your eyes forever. When you spot the Moon in the daytime, keep the Sun behind you.

Make the Moon's Phases

In a dark room, Dad takes the shade off a lamp (or sets a flashlight on a table). Push a pencil into an orange. That's your Moon, and you are Earth! Hold the Moon out at arm's length, a little above your head. First, predict: when the Moon is between you and the lamp, how much of it will look lit? Turn slowly in a circle to your left, eyes on the Moon, and watch it go from new to crescent to half to gibbous to full. Score your prediction. Bonus: draw the real Moon every night for a week and see if it matches.
